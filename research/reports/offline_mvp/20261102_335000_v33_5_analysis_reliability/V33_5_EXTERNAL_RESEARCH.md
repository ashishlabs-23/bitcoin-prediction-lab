## 2. Foundational & Contemporary Econometric Frameworks

### 2.1 The Corsi (2009) Heterogeneous Autoregressive Model of Realized Volatility (HAR-RV)

Realized volatility in speculative financial assets exhibits long memory, slow hyperbolic decay in autocorrelation functions, and multi-scale clustering (Mandelbrot, 1963; Ding, Granger, & Engle, 1993). Corsi (2009) established that true long memory is economically generated by a cascade of heterogeneous market participants operating on distinct time horizons—formalized as the **Heterogeneous Market Hypothesis** (Müller et al., 1997).

Rather than employing mathematically cumbersome fractionally integrated ARFIMA processes, Corsi (2009) proposed the additive, multi-component Heterogeneous Autoregressive model of Realized Volatility (HAR-RV). Let $RV_t^{(d)}$ denote daily realized variance constructed from high-frequency intraday log-returns:
$$RV_t^{(d)} = \sum_{j=1}^{M} r_{t,j}^2$$
Multi-period aggregated realized variances are defined as rolling averages:
$$RV_t^{(w)} = \frac{1}{d_w} \sum_{i=0}^{d_w - 1} RV_{t-i}^{(d)}, \quad RV_t^{(m)} = \frac{1}{d_m} \sum_{i=0}^{d_m - 1} RV_{t-i}^{(d)}$$
In equity markets, $d_w = 5$ and $d_m = 22$. In continuous 24/7 cryptocurrency markets, natural calendar horizons dictate $d_w = 7$ (168 hours) and $d_m = 30$ (720 hours). The standard Corsi HAR-RV regression model is formulated as:
$$RV_{t+1d}^{(d)} = c + \beta^{(d)} RV_t^{(d)} + \beta^{(w)} RV_t^{(w)} + \beta^{(m)} RV_t^{(m)} + \epsilon_{t+1d}$$
where $\epsilon_{t+1d} \sim \text{iid}(0, \sigma_\epsilon^2)$.

#### Direct Implication for BTCognitive
Corsi's structural insight confirms that volatility expectations at shorter horizons are directly conditioned by cumulative longer-horizon volatility components ($\beta^{(w)} > 0, \beta^{(m)} > 0$), whereas longer-horizon institutional traders are relatively insensitive to high-frequency intraday noise. 

BTCognitive's multi-scale architecture explicitly operationalizes this hierarchy:
- High-frequency hourly realized variance ($RV_{1h}$) captures immediate microstructure variance.
- The 24-hour forward target ($rv24h\_var\_ann$) serves as the immediate swing variance benchmark.
- The 168-hour annualized variance ($rv7d\_var\_ann$) acts as the multi-day volatility regime governor.
- Strategy execution is evaluated on aggregated 4-hour completed bars (`completed_4h_bar`), filtering out high-frequency sub-hourly whipsaws while anchoring risk parameters to 20-bar (80-hour) Donchian range boundaries.

### 2.2 Patton (2011) Loss Function Robustness & The Quasi-Likelihood (QLIKE) Criterion

A critical econometrics challenge in volatility forecasting is that the true conditional variance $\sigma_t^2 \equiv \text{Var}(r_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1})$ is a latent, unobservable variable. Empirical researchers must substitute an imperfect, observable proxy $\widehat{\sigma}_t^2$ (such as intraday realized variance, realized range, or squared returns).

Patton (2011) addressed whether ranking competing volatility models using an imperfect proxy yields the same optimal ranking as ranking them under the true latent variance $\sigma_t^2$. Patton proved that a loss function $L(\sigma^2, h)$ is **robust** to additive or multiplicative zero-mean measurement noise in the volatility proxy ($E[\widehat{\sigma}_t^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = \sigma_t^2$) if and only if it belongs to the family:
$$L(\sigma^2, h) = \frac{\sigma^{2(b+2)}}{(b+1)(b+2)} - \frac{\sigma^{2(b+1)} h}{b+1} + \frac{h^{b+2}}{b+2}$$
for parameter $b \in \mathbb{R}$. Within this family, only two loss functions are homogeneous of degree zero or degree two:
1. **Mean Squared Error (MSE)** ($b = 0$):
   $$L_{\text{MSE}}(\sigma^2, h) = (\sigma^2 - h)^2$$
2. **Quasi-Likelihood (QLIKE)** ($b = -2$):
   $$L_{\text{QLIKE}}(\sigma^2, h) = \frac{\sigma^2}{h} - \ln\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{h}\right) - 1 \quad \equiv \quad \ln(h) + \frac{\sigma^2}{h}$$
where $h$ denotes the model forecast and $\sigma^2$ is the realized variance proxy.

#### Mathematical Advantage of QLIKE over MSE in Crypto Volatility
While MSE is widely used in standard regression, Patton (2011) demonstrates that MSE is severely distorted by extreme outliers because the loss differential scales quadratically with volatility levels: $|d_{\text{MSE}}| \propto (\sigma_t^2)^2$. In Bitcoin markets, where flash crashes, liquidation cascades, and short squeezes generate realized variance spikes orders of magnitude above baseline levels, an MSE-minimizing model overfits to extreme shock periods at the expense of typical market regimes.

Conversely, QLIKE loss penalizes relative forecast errors ($\sigma^2 / h$). Taking partial derivatives reveals an essential asymmetric economic property:
$$\frac{\partial L_{\text{QLIKE}}}{\partial h} = \frac{1}{h} - \frac{\sigma^2}{h^2} = \frac{h - \sigma^2}{h^2}$$
- When a model **under-predicts** volatility ($h \ll \sigma^2$), the penalty scales with $\sigma^2 / h^2$, approaching infinity as $h \to 0$.
- When a model **over-predicts** volatility ($h \gg \sigma^2$), the penalty is bounded logarithmically by $\ln(h)$.

In risk management and trading system architecture, under-predicting volatility leads to undersized stop-losses, excessive position sizes, and catastrophic liquidation risk. Over-predicting volatility merely results in conservative sizing or abstention. Therefore, QLIKE is the mathematically and operationally superior objective function for volatility model evaluation in BTCognitive.

### 2.3 Puke & Schweikert (2024) Coherent Realized Volatility Estimation

While Patton (2011) proved that QLIKE is the optimal robust loss function for *evaluating* out-of-sample volatility forecasts, empirical research historically suffered from an internal estimation inconsistency: researchers estimated model parameters $\beta$ in-sample via Ordinary Least Squares (minimizing MSE), but evaluated the resulting models out-of-sample using QLIKE.

Puke and Schweikert (2024) solved this disconnect by embedding the HAR-RV model within the class of general M-estimators. They proved that parameter estimation should be conducted coherently using Bregman divergences, demonstrating that **qlikeHAR**—estimating the HAR model parameters $\widehat{\beta}_{\text{QLIKE}}$ directly by minimizing the empirical QLIKE loss:
$$\widehat{\beta}_{\text{QLIKE}} = \arg\min_{\beta} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left( \ln(h_t(\beta)) + \frac{RV_t}{h_t(\beta)} \right)$$
consistently outperforms OLS-estimated HAR models in out-of-sample forecasting across financial assets.

In cryptocurrency markets, where distribution tails are heavy and jump intensity is elevated, in-sample loss coherence prevents the parameter estimates $\beta^{(d)}, \beta^{(w)}, \beta^{(m)}$ from being biased by non-Gaussian residuals. BTCognitive adopts this principle: models evaluated under relative risk metrics must not rely on unconstrained quadratic error minimization during calibration.

### 2.4 Diebold–Mariano (1995) and Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold (1997) Hypothesis Testing

To determine whether a challenger model $M_1$ statistically outperforms a benchmark $M_0$, simple point comparisons of loss averages ($\bar{L}_1 < \bar{L}_0$) or nominal Sharpe ratios are methodologically invalid due to serial correlation, heteroskedasticity, and small-sample sampling variance.

Diebold and Mariano (1995) formulated a non-parametric test for predictive accuracy. Define the loss differential sequence:
$$d_t = L(y_t, \widehat{y}_{0,t}) - L(y_t, \widehat{y}_{1,t}), \quad t = 1, \dots, T$$
The null hypothesis of equal forecast accuracy is $H_0: E[d_t] = 0$. The sample mean loss differential is $\bar{d} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T d_t$. The asymptotic variance of $\bar{d}$ under $h$-step-ahead forecasting is:
$$V(\bar{d}) = \frac{1}{T} \left[ \gamma_0 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{h-1} \gamma_k \right]$$
where $\gamma_k$ is the $k$-th autocovariance of $d_t$, estimated using the Newey and West (1987) Bartlett spectral kernel. The Diebold–Mariano test statistic is:
$$S_{\text{DM}} = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\widehat{V}(\bar{d})}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

#### The HLN Finite-Sample Correction
Harvey, Leybourne, and Newbold (1997) demonstrated that the asymptotic DM test severely over-rejects the null hypothesis in finite samples ($T < 250$), exhibiting substantial size distortion when forecast errors exhibit autocorrelation or non-normality. HLN introduced the finite-sample corrected test statistic:
$$S_{\text{HLN}} = S_{\text{DM}} \times \left[ \frac{T + 1 - 2h + h(h-1)/T}{T} \right]^{1/2}$$
Harvey et al. (1997) proved that $S_{\text{HLN}}$ must be compared against the critical values of **Student’s $t$-distribution with $(T - 1)$ degrees of freedom**, rather than the standard normal distribution.

In BTCognitive, this standard is non-negotiable. AGENTS.md Phase 5 explicitly mandates that any empirical bake-off between competing strategies or variance models must execute the HLN-corrected DM test. If $p \ge 0.05$ under $t_{T-1}$, the null hypothesis of equal performance cannot be rejected, and the challenger remains classified as `RESEARCH_ONLY` without production authorization.

### 2.5 Hansen, Lunde, & Nason (2011) Model Confidence Set (MCS)

When comparing multiple strategies or volatility models concurrently (e.g., in BTCognitive’s Strategy Tournament consisting of Donchian Breakouts, Bollinger Reversion, ATR Volatility Expansion, Buy-and-Hold, and Matched Random Entry), pairwise DM tests suffer from severe multi-hypothesis testing data snooping (White, 2000; Sullivan, Timmermann, & White, 1999).

Hansen, Lunde, and Nason (2011) introduced the **Model Confidence Set (MCS)** procedure. Rather than seeking a single "optimal" model—which may be an artifact of sample-specific noise—the MCS determines the subset of models $\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^*_{1-\alpha} \subset \mathcal{M}_0$ that contains the best model with a pre-specified confidence level $1 - \alpha$ (typically 90% or 95%).

The MCS algorithm proceeds iteratively:
1. Formulate the null hypothesis of equal predictive ability across all models currently in set $\mathcal{M}$:
   $$H_{0,\mathcal{M}}: E[d_{i,j,t}] = 0 \quad \forall i, j \in \mathcal{M}$$
   where $d_{i,j,t} = L_{i,t} - L_{j,t}$.
2. Construct the test statistic $T_{\text{max},\mathcal{M}} = \max_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \frac{\bar{d}_{i\cdot}}{\widehat{\text{se}}(\bar{d}_{i\cdot})}$, where $\bar{d}_{i\cdot} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|-1}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}} \bar{d}_{i,j}$.
3. Evaluate the distribution of $T_{\text{max},\mathcal{M}}$ via the stationary block bootstrap (Politis & Romano, 1994) to preserve serial dependence.
4. If $H_{0,\mathcal{M}}$ is rejected at significance level $\alpha$, remove the worst-performing model using the elimination rule $e_{\mathcal{M}} = \arg\max_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \frac{\bar{d}_{i\cdot}}{\widehat{\text{se}}(\bar{d}_{i\cdot})}$, and repeat until the hypothesis cannot be rejected.

The MCS acknowledges the fundamental reality of financial econometrics: in many periods, market data is insufficiently informative to distinguish between the top two or three candidate models. BTCognitive's Strategy Tournament embraces this by selecting the top-two candidates for subsequent scenario evaluation, rather than naively locking into a single overfitted winner.

## 3. Bitcoin Market Microstructure, Breakout Dynamics, & Execution Frictions

### 3.1 Empirical Evidence on Technical Breakouts in Bitcoin
The academic literature evaluating technical trading rules on Bitcoin presents consistent, rigorous findings regarding breakout strategies:

1. **Gerritsen, Bouri, Ramezanifar, & Roubaud (2020)** (*Finance Research Letters*):
   Evaluated seven trend-following technical trading rules on daily Bitcoin series from 2010 through 2019. They established that **trading range breakout rules** demonstrated substantial predictive power during sustained bull expansions, outperforming buy-and-hold on a Sharpe-ratio basis. However, their predictive power decayed rapidly during consolidation and bear regimes, demonstrating extreme regime dependency.

2. **Corbet, Eraslan, Lucey, & Şensoy (2019)** (*Finance Research Letters*):
   Analyzed high-frequency cryptocurrency data to test the profitability of variable-length moving averages and breakout rules. They demonstrated that while technical trading rules capture persistent intraday momentum, buy signals and sell signals exhibit significant return asymmetry. Short breakout rules systematically underperformed due to structural upward drift and violent short-covering liquidation spikes.

3. **Deprez & Frömmel (2024)** (*International Review of Economics & Finance*):
   Conducted a large-scale evaluation of over 7,000 technical trading rules across Bitcoin's historical record. After implementing White's (2000) Reality Check and Hansen's (2005) Superior Predictive Ability (SPA) test to adjust for data snooping, their primary finding was stark: **virtually all apparent gross profitability in simple technical rules completely disappears once realistic exchange transaction costs and slippage are incorporated**. Strategies with high turnover bleed capital continuously to trading venues.

### 3.2 False Breakout Rates and Liquidity Sweeps
In traditional equity and FX markets, Donchian channel breakouts ($N = 20$) exhibit empirical failure rates (false breakouts) of 50% to 58%. In Bitcoin markets, false breakout rates on intraday timeframes (15m, 1h) routinely exceed **60% to 68%**.

This heightened failure rate is driven by crypto-specific market microstructure:
- **Liquidity Clustering & Stop Runs:** Leveraged retail traders cluster stop-loss orders immediately outside visible local swing highs and lows. Market makers and algorithmic proprietary desks actively sweep these liquidity pools to fill large inventory orders (Makarov & Schoar, 2020; Alexander et al., 2020).
- **Wick Reversals:** A high-frequency price surge may pierce the 20-bar Donchian high during an hourly candle, only to retrace completely before candle close, leaving a long upper shadow (wick) and trapping breakout buyers at the local peak.
- **Regime Vulnerability:** In mean-reverting (`RANGE_BOUND`) or downtrending (`TRENDING_BEARISH`) regimes, Donchian breaches are almost exclusively exhaustion moves or liquidity traps rather than sustained trend initiations.

### 3.3 Microstructure Execution Frictions & Cost Schedules
Academic backtests frequently assume frictionless execution or unrealistically low fees (e.g., 2–5 bps). In physical reality, cryptocurrency execution costs comprise three non-trivial components:

1. **Exchange Maker/Taker Fees:**
   - Tier-1 institutional accounts typically achieve 5–15 bps round-trip fees.
   - Retail exchange accounts (e.g., standard Coinbase, Kraken, or Binance spot tiers) incur 40 to 60 bps taker fees per leg, resulting in 80 to 120 bps round-trip friction.
2. **Bid-Ask Spread:**
   - Under normal market conditions, BTC/USD top-of-book spread is tight ($0.01 to $1.00, or 0.1 to 1.5 bps).
   - During volatility expansions and breakouts—precisely when trend-following strategies trigger—spreads widen dramatically to 5–15 bps.
3. **Execution Slippage & Adverse Selection:**
   - When a breakout triggers, systematic market orders compete for liquidity on the order book. Taker orders walk the book, incurring 5 to 25 bps of adverse slippage relative to the trigger candle's close.

BTCognitive enforces a strict institutional cost schedule in all retrospective and prospective evaluations:
- **Standard Institutional Schedule:** 26.0 bps round-trip (16.0 bps taker fees + 10.0 bps bid-ask spread and slippage allowance).
- **Retail Stress Schedule:** 87.0 bps round-trip (Coinbase retail taker fee schedule).

Any strategy whose gross expectancy is less than the cost hurdle is economically bankrupt, regardless of its win rate.

### 4.1 Econometric Analysis of the Empirical Findings

#### 1. Realism of the 48.18% Win Rate
In trend-following and breakout trading, a win rate between 40% and 50% is standard and mathematically sound. When trading with an asymmetric reward-to-risk profile (e.g., Take Profit at +1.5R and Stop Loss at -1.0R), the mathematical breakeven win rate $p^*$ net of friction is:
$$p^* = \frac{1 + \text{CostRatio}}{1 + \text{RR}}$$
For $RR = 1.5$ and nominal stop $1.50\%$ ($150\text{ bps}$):
$$p^* = \frac{150 + 26}{150 \times 1.5 + 150} = \frac{176}{375} \approx 46.93\%$$
BTCognitive's retained subgroup achieves **48.18%**, which cleanly exceeds the breakeven hurdle, generating a robust positive net expectancy of **+59.25 bps per trade**. Conversely, retail marketing claims of "90% win rates" are mathematically impossible in liquid markets unless the strategy employs negative skewness (e.g., risking $10 to make $1, where a single tail loss wipes out 50 consecutive wins).

#### 2. Abstention as the Primary Engine of Alpha
The unfiltered breakout population yielded 194 trades with a marginal net expectancy of **+9.57 bps**—a razor-thin edge that would likely degrade to negative returns under retail fee structures. 

However, by enforcing the **Multi-Timeframe Regime Classifier**, BTCognitive partitioned the 194 trades into:
- **110 Retained Trades:** Win rate **48.18%**, net expectancy **+59.25 bps**.
- **84 Excluded Trades:** Win rate **38.10%**, net expectancy **-55.49 bps**.

The excluded trades represent false breakouts occurring in `TRENDING_BEARISH` or `RANGE_BOUND` regimes. **Excluding these 84 toxic trades eliminated a cumulative loss of -4,661 basis points from the strategy ledger.** This directly validates the theoretical predictions of Deprez and Frömmel (2024): the economic value of algorithmic trading in crypto does not originate from entering every breakout, but from systematic, cost-aware **abstention** during hostile market regimes.

### 5.1 Why Decoupling is Essential
In monolithic or poorly architected trading systems, an API call returning `status: "SUCCESS"` is conflated with an active trading signal. This leads to severe operational errors:
1. **Misleading Stop References:** If an analysis engine detects a market regime but finds no active breakout, returning the Donchian channel low as a default `stop_reference` misleads operators or automated systems into believing an active position with a protective stop exists. Under BTCognitive's decoupled contract, for any inactive or blocked setup (`setup_status: "BLOCKED"`, `"NO_SETUP"`, `"SETUP_FORMING"`), `entry_reference`, `stop_reference`, and `target_reference` are strictly `null` (rendered as `"Unavailable"` in dashboards).
2. **Stale Snapshot Presentation:** Market intelligence calculation is deterministic and can evaluate successfully on historical or replayed data. However, a setup eligibility evaluation must enforce a **Data Freshness SLA**. If candle data is stale ($> 2.0\text{ hours}$ old), Layer 1 succeeds with `status: "SUCCESS"`, but Layer 2 strictly sets `setup_status: "BLOCKED"` with reason code `STALE_MARKET_DATA_BLOCKED`. Stale market data cannot authorize live setups.
3. **Regime Incompatibility:** The strategy `HTF_RANGE_EXPANSION_BREAKOUT` is long-only. If Layer 1 detects a `TRENDING_BEARISH` regime (e.g., $Close < EMA_{50} < EMA_{200}$ with $ADX \ge 20$), Layer 1 operates correctly, but Layer 2 rejects setup formation (`REGIME_MISMATCH_BEARISH`). The system never forces an entry in an incompatible regime.
